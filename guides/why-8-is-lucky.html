It starts with a sound

Chinese is rich in near-homophones: words that share sounds but differ in tone or meaning. Numbers are short and spoken constantly, so they pick up associations quickly. Eight in Mandarin is bā. It rhymes with fā (发), the first syllable of fācái, "to get rich". In Cantonese, spoken across Hong Kong, Guangdong and much of the diaspora, eight is baat, which sounds close to faat — the same idea of prosperity.

That rhyme is the whole engine. There is no ancient decree that 8 is lucky; there is a sound that resembles a wish, repeated billions of times a day in prices, phone numbers and conversation. Over generations, an association became a convention, and a convention became a market.

Shape, balance and the "widening" eight

The written character 八 adds a second layer. Its two strokes spread apart as they descend, which in Japan is read as suehirogari — a fan opening outward, a fortune that grows over time. That is why 8 is favoured in Japan too, even though the Japanese pronunciation (hachi) carries no money rhyme. In Western numerology, 8 is independently linked with ambition, authority and material success, and its looping shape is often compared to the infinity symbol. Three traditions, three different reasons, one shared conclusion — which partly explains why 8 travels so well globally.

Where the belief turns into money

The most famous example is a date. The Beijing Olympics opened at 8:08 pm on the 8th day of the 8th month of 2008. The choice was deliberate and widely reported as a nod to good fortune.

The clearest price signals come from scarce numbered assets:

  • Phone numbers. In 2020 a Beijing court auctioned a mobile number ending in five eights. More than 5,000 bids pushed the price to 2.25 million yuan, roughly US$300,000. Reports at the time noted that companies in particular pay premiums for such numbers to impress clients.
  • Licence plates. In Hong Kong, plates such as "18" and "28" — read as "will prosper" and "easy prosperity" — have each sold for around US$2 million.
  • Domain names. Numeric .com domains became a distinct asset class in the mid-2010s, driven largely by Chinese buyers. Dealers price an 8 as a premium digit and a 4 as a defect.
  • Property. Developers in Chinese-speaking cities favour 8 in unit numbers and frequently relabel or skip floors containing 4.

What businesses do with it

Once you notice it, you see 8 everywhere in commerce aimed at Chinese-speaking customers:

  • Prices ending in 8 (¥88, ¥168, $888) instead of the Western charm ending of 9.
  • Launch dates on the 8th, 18th or 28th, or in the 8th month.
  • Product and model numbers with 8 and without 4.
  • Phrases like 168 (yī lù fā, "prosper all the way") and 518 (wǒ yào fā, "I will prosper") in shop names and hotlines.

For a Western brand entering these markets, the cost of following the convention is close to zero and the cost of ignoring it — say, launching a "4-series" product or a $44 price point — can be a quiet drag on conversion.

Limits and counter-examples

Symbolism is not destiny, and it is not uniform. Not every Chinese-speaking customer cares; younger urban buyers often treat it as a playful tradition rather than a rule. Prices for numeric domains surged in 2015–2016 and then corrected, a reminder that cultural premiums can behave like any speculative market. And meanings depend on context: in online chat, 88 also simply means "bye-bye".

Other traditions disagree on 8, too. In Vedic numerology the number is ruled by Saturn and associated with discipline, delay and karma — respected, but not simply "lucky". If your audience is in India, 8 is not an automatic win.

Practical takeaways

  1. For Chinese-speaking audiences, prefer 8, 6 and 9; avoid 4, especially at the end of a number.
  2. Treat number choices as localisation, like translation — test per market.
  3. If you own a number heavy in 8s, price it for the market that values it most, not the one you happen to live in.
